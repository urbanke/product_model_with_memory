The retained output alphabet $V$ and the resolutions of the two lags are
three separate controls.  We use the state
\[
 \bigl(b_{M_1}(z_{t-1}),b_{M_2}(z_{t-2})\bigr),
 \qquad 0\leq M_2\leq M_1\leq V,
\]
where $b_M$ gives individual states to $M$ retained symbols and pools the
rest.  Both state sets follow the same corpus-frequency order, so they are
nested.  The decoder is told them with
\[
 \log_2\binom{V}{M_1}+\log_2\binom{M_1}{M_2}
\]
bits, in addition to the vocabulary, escaped-identity, and declared
model-grid charges.  Every data-bearing successor sequence, including
fallback and escape-payload sequences, is coded by
\texttt{layered\_depth\_averaged\_product\_simplex\_v1}.  The convention
$M_2=0$ makes the second component constant and must reproduce the
corresponding first-order member exactly; this identity is a hard numerical
gate before an order-two result is accepted.  All rates divide the complete
code length by the number of bytes in the original corpus.

The direct second lag does not improve the best code on text8 or enwik8:
their winners remain the exact $M_2=0$ first-order members.  On enwik9 the
larger sample supports a genuine second-lag gain: $M_2=512$ improves on its
$1.828580231$-bpc $M_2=0$ reference by $0.097956731$ bpc.  The gain depends
strongly on allocating capacity jointly across $V$, $M_1$, and $M_2$, rather
than fixing the full vocabulary.  Appendix~\ref{app:memory-two-results}
lists every distinct completed experiment, one table per corpus, ordered
lexicographically by $(V,M_1,M_2)$ rather than by performance.
